**Clinical Roleplay Scenario: Reality Testing with a Genderfluid Client Experiencing Depressive Symptoms**

**Session Context:** The client, Jamie, is a 20-year-old genderfluid individual who has been experiencing depressive symptoms following a history of bullying. Jamie uses they/them pronouns. This is their third session with the therapist.

**Opening Phase:**

1. Therapist: Good morning, Jamie. Thanks for coming in today. How are you feeling?
2. Client: (sighs) Honestly, not great. Just feeling really down and stuck. Everything feels pointless.

3. Therapist: I'm sorry to hear that. It sounds like things are really tough right now. Can you tell me more about what's been going on and how you've been feeling since our last session?
4. Client: (shrugs) Just the same stuff. People still staring at me on the street, making comments. I feel like I'm just a freak to them. And it's hard to get out of bed in the morning.

5. Therapist: That must be really hard for you. It sounds like the bullying and negative attention are still affecting you quite a bit. Before we dive into that, I want to check in on something. You mentioned feeling like everything is pointless. Can you think of anything that you've enjoyed or found meaningful recently, no matter how small it might seem?
6. Client: (pauses) I guess I did enjoy a video game I played over the weekend. It was just something to take my mind off things.

**Middle Phase:**

7. Therapist: That's great that you were able to find some enjoyment in that. It might seem small, but it's a good sign. Now, let's talk about these feelings of being a "freak." Can you think of times when people have stared or made comments, but you didn't let it get to you as much?
8. Client: (hesitates) I guess when I'm with my close friends, it doesn't bother me as much. They're really supportive.

9. Therapist: That's really positive. It sounds like having a supportive network makes a big difference. Let's use reality testing to examine these negative thoughts you're having. Can you tell me a specific negative thought that's been on your mind lately?
10. Client: (hesitates, then says) "I'm worthless because I'm different."

11. Therapist: Okay, let's examine this thought. What's the evidence for and against the idea that you're worthless because you're different?
12. Client: (thinks) I guess the evidence for it is all the times people have made me feel bad about being different. But against it...I don't know, it just feels like there's not much.

13. Therapist: Let's explore that further. Can you think of any experiences or interactions that contradict the idea that you're worthless?
14. Client: (slowly) My friends care about me, and they like me for who I am. And there are online communities where I can be myself without being judged.

15. Therapist: Exactly. Those are significant pieces of evidence against the thought that you're worthless. It sounds like there are people who value and accept you for who you are. How does that make you feel?
16. Client: (tearfully) It makes me feel a bit better, I guess. Like, maybe I'm not as alone as I feel.

17. Therapist: That's a really important realization. Feeling connected and valued by others can be a powerful counter to feelings of worthlessness. Let's also consider this: just because some people have made you feel bad about being different, does that mean that their opinions are objectively true or valid?
18. Client: (hesitates) I guess not. It just feels like they're right sometimes.

19. Therapist: It's understandable that their negative opinions can feel impactful, but it's also important to remember that their views are often a reflection of their own biases and issues, not yours. Can you think of a time when someone's negative comment was clearly not about you, but about their own discomfort or prejudice?
20. Client: (nods) Yeah, there was this one time someone yelled a slur at me. It was clear they were angry and hateful, not that I was doing anything wrong.

21. Therapist: That example illustrates how others' negative reactions can be more about them than about you. Let's keep working with this reality testing approach to challenge some of these negative thoughts and see if we can develop a more balanced view of yourself and your worth.

**Closing Phase:**

22. Therapist: Before we wrap up today, what are your thoughts on what we've discussed? Is there anything that stood out to you or felt particularly helpful?
23. Client: (reflectively) I guess it was helpful to think about the times when people have been supportive. It made me realize I'm not as alone as I feel.

24. Therapist: That's great. Remember, it's okay to feel overwhelmed, and it's a process to work through these feelings. We'll continue to explore these issues in our next sessions. For now, what are some coping strategies or things you can do for yourself between now and our next session?
25. Client: (thinks) I can try to reach out to my friends more and maybe get back into that video game.

26. Therapist: Those are great ideas. Reconnecting with supportive friends and engaging in activities you enjoy can be really helpful. Let's make a note to follow up on those plans in our next session. Thanks for sharing your experiences with me today, Jamie. I'm here to support you.
27. Client: (nods, slightly more hopeful) Thanks.

This scenario demonstrates the use of reality testing as a therapeutic technique to help Jamie challenge negative thoughts and perceptions related to their experiences of bullying and current depressive symptoms. By examining the evidence for and against their negative self-view and highlighting supportive relationships and experiences, the therapist aims to help Jamie develop a more balanced and compassionate self-image.
